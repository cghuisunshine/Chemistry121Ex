Properties of Molecules and Macromolecules

Connect bonding and intermolecular forces to phase changes, solids, polymers, and sustainability.

Intermolecular forcesSolidsPolymer structure

11.1 Learning Objectives

Workbook reference · page 11.2

Identify intermolecular attractions, explain trends in boiling and solubility, classify solids, compare polymer architectures, and connect molecular structure to mechanical properties. Evaluate environmental claims using material composition, use conditions, and realistic end-of-life pathways.

11.2 Physical Properties

Workbook reference · page 11.3

Physical properties arise from interactions among particles and the ways those particles are arranged. Begin with structure: identify charges, polarity, polarizability, hydrogen-bonding groups, and whether the material consists of discrete molecules or an extended bonded network.

A phase change usually rearranges interactions between particles. Boiling molecular water separates H₂O molecules; it does not ordinarily break their O–H covalent bonds. Decomposition is a chemical change and may occur before some materials melt or boil.

11.2.1 Boiling and Melting

Workbook reference · page 11.3

A liquid boils when its vapour pressure equals the external pressure. Stronger cohesive attractions usually lower vapour pressure at a fixed temperature and raise the boiling temperature at a fixed pressure. Reducing external pressure lowers the boiling temperature.

Melting depends on both interactions and crystal packing. Molecular symmetry, shape, and packing can make melting-point comparisons less straightforward than boiling-point comparisons. Do not assume that a substance with the highest boiling point must also have the highest melting point.

Worked comparison: ethanol and dimethyl ether

Both have formula C₂H₆O and comparable molar mass. Ethanol molecules can donate and accept hydrogen bonds. Dimethyl ether can accept them from a donor such as water but cannot donate them to other ether molecules. Ethanol therefore has stronger self-association and a substantially higher boiling point.

11.2.2 Solubility

Workbook reference · page 11.4

Dissolution separates solute particles and reorganizes solvent particles, then forms solute–solvent interactions. Favourability depends on the overall Gibbs energy change, ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, rather than one attractive interaction alone. “Like dissolves like” is a useful qualitative starting point.

Polar and ionic solutes often interact favourably with polar solvents, while nonpolar solutes often mix better with nonpolar solvents. A small alcohol's OH group can support water solubility, but a longer hydrocarbon chain increases its nonpolar contribution. Strong ionic lattice attractions can make an ionic solid sparingly soluble even in water.

11.2.3 Interatomic and Intermolecular Forces

Workbook reference · page 11.5

  • London dispersion: instantaneous and induced dipoles; present in all atoms and molecules. Greater polarizability and effective contact often strengthen it.
  • Dipole–dipole interactions: attractions involving permanent molecular dipoles; orientation matters.
  • Hydrogen bonding: in common introductory examples, H attached to N, O, or F interacts with an appropriate lone-pair acceptor.
  • Ion–dipole interactions: an ion interacts with a polar molecule, as in hydration.
  • Ionic and network covalent bonding: extended interactions that require distinguishing a lattice or network from a collection of discrete molecules.

Worked comparison: pentane isomers

Less compact n-pentane can make more effective intermolecular contact than compact neopentane. With the same formula and no hydrogen bonding, differences in dispersion contact help explain n-pentane's higher boiling point. Molar mass alone cannot distinguish the isomers.

Common mistake

Do not assign only one force to a polar molecule. Dispersion is still present alongside permanent dipole interactions and any hydrogen bonding. The dominant comparison depends on the particular species.

11.3 Types of Solids

Workbook reference · page 11.12

Classify a solid by its constituent particles and the forces linking them. Ionic solids contain ions; molecular solids contain discrete molecules; network covalent solids contain extended covalent bonding. Metallic solids are another important category, with delocalized electrons supporting metallic bonding.

Classification predicts patterns but has exceptions and structural subtleties. Ask what would have to be disrupted to melt the material, what particles could move, and whether mobile charge carriers exist.

11.3.1 Ionic Solids

Workbook reference · page 11.12

Cations and anions form an electrically neutral lattice. These solids are often hard, brittle, and high-melting. Shifting layers can bring like charges close together, promoting fracture rather than easy plastic deformation.

An ordinary ionic crystal does not conduct well because its ions are fixed in position. When molten, or when dissolved sufficiently in a solvent, mobile ions can carry current. Do not confuse dissolution with melting or assume every ionic compound is highly soluble.

11.3.2 Molecular Solids

Workbook reference · page 11.15

Molecular solids retain discrete molecules linked by intermolecular attractions. Examples include solid CO₂ and ice. Melting primarily disrupts the arrangement of molecules rather than breaking the internal covalent framework of each molecule.

Their melting points and hardness depend on the strength and geometry of the intermolecular interactions and on packing. Molecular solids are generally poor electrical conductors unless additional charge carriers are introduced.

11.3.3 Network Covalent Solids

Workbook reference · page 11.15

An extended covalent network can produce high hardness and high melting or decomposition temperatures. Diamond links carbon atoms in a three-dimensional tetrahedral network. SiO₂ contains a network of Si–O connectivity rather than discrete SiO₂ molecules in ordinary quartz.

Graphite shows why structure matters: strong covalent bonding exists within sheets, while weaker interactions connect the sheets. Delocalized electrons support conductivity within the layers, and the layers can slide. “Network solid” does not mean every property is the same in every direction.

11.4 Examples of Solids

Workbook reference · page 11.15

Compare four structural pictures

  • NaCl: ionic lattice; mobile ions appear upon melting.
  • Ice: molecular solid with an extended hydrogen-bond arrangement; O–H bonds remain within H₂O.
  • Diamond: three-dimensional covalent network; very hard and an electrical insulator under ordinary conditions.
  • Graphite: covalent layers with delocalized electrons and weaker interlayer interactions; conductive and readily sheared along layers.

Use these comparisons to explain properties from structure rather than memorizing a label. Diamond and graphite contain the same element but have different bonding arrangements and therefore very different properties.

11.5 Polymers

Workbook reference · page 11.21

A polymer consists of macromolecules containing many connected repeat units. A monomer is a starting molecular building block; a repeat unit is the pattern repeated in the finished chain. They are related but are not always identical formulas because some polymerizations eliminate small molecules.

For polyethylene, addition across ethene C=C bonds produces a chain represented as [–CH₂–CH₂–]ₙ. The backbone contains C–C single bonds, not the original series of monomer double bonds. Chain length, distribution of chain lengths, intermolecular attractions, and packing all influence properties.

11.5.1 Linear, Branched, and Crosslinked Polymers

Workbook reference · page 11.23

Linear polymers have long backbones without substantial branching. Branched polymers contain side chains that can hinder close packing. Crosslinked polymers have covalent links joining chains, restricting relative motion and potentially forming a network.

In comparable polyethylene materials, greater branching often reduces efficient packing and crystallinity. Light crosslinking can help an elastomer recover after stretching; extensive crosslinking tends to produce a more rigid network. Thermoplastics can soften and flow on heating within useful limits; heavily crosslinked thermosets do not simply melt and flow.

11.5.2 Silicone: Preparation of Silicones

Workbook reference · page 11.28

Silicones typically have Si–O–Si backbones with organic substituents on silicon. A common repeat unit is –Si(CH₃)₂–O–. Silicone is a polymer family; silicon is the element, and silica is a network silicon oxide. These names are not interchangeable.

One illustrative route hydrolyzes a difunctional chlorosilane: (CH₃)₂SiCl₂ + 2H₂O → (CH₃)₂Si(OH)₂ + 2HCl. Condensation of silanol groups then forms Si–O–Si links while releasing water. Functional-group count controls whether chains or more connected networks are favoured. This reaction describes the chemistry; any laboratory preparation requires the course's specified procedure and controls.

11.6 Sustainability: Environmental Issues Related to Polymers

Workbook reference · page 11.31

Evaluate polymers across extraction, synthesis, transport, use, reuse, and end of life. A material can save energy during use yet pose difficult waste-management problems. Compare the same service, such as delivering a specified amount of product, rather than comparing arbitrary masses of different materials.

Useful questions include: what feedstock and energy were used, how long does the item function, can it be collected and separated, and what happens in the actual disposal environment? A single label rarely answers all of them.

11.6.1 Feedstock Issues

Workbook reference · page 11.31

Feedstocks may be fossil-derived or bio-based. Renewable origin does not automatically imply lower total impact: cultivation, land use, processing, and energy inputs also matter. Conversely, recycled feedstock can reduce demand for newly extracted material while still requiring collection and processing.

Separate the source of carbon from end-of-life behaviour. A bio-based polymer may be persistent, and a polymer's ability to biodegrade does not by itself identify whether its feedstock was renewable.

11.6.2 Polymer Recycling

Workbook reference · page 11.31

Mechanical recycling sorts, cleans, and reprocesses material while largely preserving polymer identity. Chemical recycling uses reactions to change the polymer, sometimes recovering monomers or other feedstocks. Both require energy and sufficiently suitable input streams.

Mixed polymer types, additives, contamination, and degradation can limit recovered quality. A resin-identification symbol identifies a material category; it does not guarantee acceptance by a particular local collection system. Evaluate actual recovery and product quality rather than theoretical recyclability alone.

11.6.3 Polymers and Pollution

Workbook reference · page 11.33

Persistent polymer waste can accumulate and fragment into smaller particles. Fragmentation changes particle size without necessarily converting the polymer into simple metabolic products. Additives and transport through environmental systems add further complexity.

Distinguish preventing release from managing material after release. Durable design, reuse, collection, and suitable waste treatment address different stages. Claims about a material's environmental fate need evidence under specified conditions.

11.6.4 Biodegradable Polymers

Workbook reference · page 11.33

Biodegradation involves biological conversion under particular conditions and over a specified time. Temperature, water, oxygen, microbes, and polymer chemistry all matter. Industrial compostability does not imply rapid breakdown in cold seawater or in a home compost pile.

Some hydrolysable polymer backbones can be cleaved into smaller units, but cleavage alone is not proof of complete biodegradation. Compare a claim's test environment with the environment where the product will actually end up.

11.6.5 Polymers in Biological Systems

Workbook reference · page 11.34

Proteins, polysaccharides, and nucleic acids are biological macromolecules. Proteins contain amino-acid residues joined by peptide bonds; polysaccharides link sugar residues through glycosidic bonds. Their sequence and three-dimensional organization control function.

Hydrogen bonding, ionic interactions, dispersion, and hydrophobic effects help organize biological structures. Some proteins also contain covalent disulfide crosslinks. A macromolecule's biological origin does not remove the need to analyze its actual bonding and interactions.

11.6.6 Nucleic Acids

Workbook reference · page 11.34

DNA and RNA consist of nucleotide units linked through phosphodiester backbones. A nucleotide includes a sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogen-containing base. Sequence stores information; complementary interactions allow specific recognition.

In the familiar DNA duplex, A pairs with T and G with C. Hydrogen bonding supports pairing specificity, while base stacking and the solvent environment also contribute to stability. The backbones are covalently linked along each strand; the complementary strands are not ordinarily joined by a continuous series of covalent base-pair bonds.

↗ Practice questions

Why does molten NaCl conduct while solid NaCl usually does not?Think first, then open the explanation

In the molten material, ions can move and transport charge. In the solid lattice, the ions are constrained near fixed positions. The explanation concerns ion mobility, not the appearance of free metallic electrons.

Does a larger molecule always have a higher boiling point?Think first, then open the explanation

No. Greater size often increases polarizability, but hydrogen bonding, shape, polarity, and other factors can change a comparison. Identify comparable families before applying a size trend.

A bag is bio-based and industrially compostable. What is still unknown?Think first, then open the explanation

Its impacts during production and use, whether it will be collected into suitable composting, and how it behaves in other environments remain unknown. Neither label establishes rapid degradation in every setting or the lowest life-cycle impact.
