# Chemical Bonding and Lewis Structures
> Build an electron-counting model, then use it to explain bond strength, charge, and resonance.

## 2.1 Learning Objectives
By the end of this chapter, you should be able to count valence electrons in molecules and ions, draw and compare Lewis structures, assign formal charges and oxidation states, and explain bond polarity and resonance. These are different descriptions of the same species: always say which model you are using.

## 2.2 Chemical Bonding
A chemical bond forms when the bonded arrangement has lower energy than the separated constituents under the comparison being made. Attraction between electrons and nuclei competes with electron–electron and nucleus–nucleus repulsion. At the equilibrium bond length, the potential energy is at a minimum; pushing atoms closer eventually increases repulsion sharply.

Breaking a bond requires energy; forming a bond releases energy. Whether a whole reaction releases energy depends on all bonds broken and formed, along with other changes in the system.

### Worked example: reading a bond-energy curve
If separated atoms are assigned energy zero and the minimum is at −436 kJ mol⁻¹, dissociation from that minimum requires approximately +436 kJ mol⁻¹. The horizontal position of the minimum gives the equilibrium bond length, not the bond energy.

## 2.2.1 Ionic Compounds
An ionic solid contains cations and anions in an extended lattice, rather than separate molecules of its formula unit. NaCl records a 1:1 ion ratio. MgCl₂ records Mg²⁺ and two Cl⁻ ions, giving overall electrical neutrality.

Electrostatic attraction grows with the product of charge magnitudes and decreases with separation. The simple pair potential is `U = k q₁q₂/r`: opposite charges give a negative interaction energy. Lattice energies also depend on crystal structure and repulsions, so the pair expression alone is not a complete solid-state calculation.

## 2.2.2 Covalent Bonding and Lewis Structures of Diatomic Molecules
A Lewis structure represents shared electron pairs as bonds and nonbonding pairs as dots. H₂ shares one pair; each H then has a two-electron shell. F₂ has one F–F bond and three lone pairs on each fluorine. O₂ is represented by a double bond and two lone pairs per oxygen; N₂ has a triple bond and one lone pair per nitrogen.

Count every electron once in the total. The Lewis model is useful for connectivity and electron bookkeeping, but it does not explain O₂'s observed paramagnetism; molecular orbital theory will do that in Chapter 8.

## 2.2.3 Bond Order and Bond Length
In an individual Lewis contributor, single, double, and triple bonds have bond orders 1, 2, and 3. For comparable bonds between the same elements, increasing bond order generally shortens and strengthens the bond: C≡C is shorter than C=C, which is shorter than C–C.

Do not compare unrelated atom pairs by bond order alone. Atomic size and chemical environment also affect length and strength. Equivalent resonance contributors can give an average bond order, such as 1.5 for the two C–O bonds of a carboxylate ion.

## 2.2.4 Polar Covalent Bonds
Electronegativity describes an atom's tendency to attract bonding electron density. In H–Cl the electron density is shifted toward Cl, so H is δ⁺ and Cl is δ⁻. These partial charges do not mean that the molecule contains fully separated H⁺ and Cl⁻ ions.

Bond dipoles are vectors. Polar bonds can cancel in a symmetric molecule, so bond polarity alone cannot establish molecular polarity. Chapter 3 combines bond dipoles with three-dimensional shape.

## 2.2.5 Percent Ionic Character
One experimental estimate compares the measured bond dipole moment with the moment expected for complete separation of one elementary charge across the bond length: `percent ionic character = 100 × μ(measured)/(e r)`. Use consistent units: μ in C m, e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, and r in metres. One debye is approximately 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ C m.

For an illustrative bond with μ = 1.00 D and r = 100 pm, the fully ionic moment would be 1.602 × 10⁻²⁹ C m, giving about 20.8%. This is a model-dependent estimate of polarity, not a literal fraction of molecules that are ionic.

## 2.3 Lewis Structures of Polyatomic Species
Use the same procedure each time:

1. Add the valence electrons of all atoms; add electrons for a negative charge and subtract for a positive charge.
2. Choose a plausible skeleton. H is terminal; the less electronegative atom is often central.
3. Place single bonds, complete terminal shells, and put remaining electrons on the central atom.
4. If a second-period central atom lacks an octet, consider converting adjacent lone pairs into multiple bonds.
5. Check the total electron count and the sum of formal charges.

### Worked example: CO₂
The total is 4 + 2(6) = 16 electrons. Starting O–C–O and completing terminal octets uses all 16, but leaves carbon with only four surrounding electrons. Convert one lone pair from each oxygen into a bond to give O=C=O. Carbon and both oxygens have octets, and all formal charges are zero.

## 2.3.1 Octet Rule
Many main-group atoms are particularly stable with eight surrounding valence electrons. Hydrogen follows a duet rule. Carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, and fluorine cannot exceed an octet in ordinary Lewis structures.

Exceptions include electron-deficient BF₃ and odd-electron NO. Some third-period and heavier species are drawn with expanded valence shells, such as SF₆. These Lewis drawings are bookkeeping models; expanded shells should not automatically be explained as strong participation of d orbitals in hybridization.

## 2.3.2 Formal Charge
Formal charge splits every bond's electrons equally: `FC = V − N − B/2`, where V is the free atom's valence-electron count, N is its number of nonbonding electrons, and B is its number of bonding electrons. The formal charges must add to the overall species charge.

### Worked example: NH₄⁺
There are 5 + 4(1) − 1 = 8 valence electrons. Four N–H single bonds use all eight. Nitrogen has FC = 5 − 0 − 8/2 = +1; each H has FC = 1 − 0 − 2/2 = 0. Draw brackets and the overall + charge.

Prefer reasonable structures with small formal-charge magnitudes and, when other factors are comparable, negative charge on more electronegative atoms. Satisfying second-period octets takes priority over simply forcing all formal charges to zero.

## 2.3.3 Drawing Lewis Structures for More Complicated Species
Keep a written electron budget. In ions, charge changes the electron count, not the number of atoms. For species with several central atoms, establish connectivity first, then complete each atom's valence shell. A formula alone may permit multiple constitutional isomers, so use any supplied structural information.

For HCN, 1 + 4 + 5 = 10 electrons give H–C≡N with one lone pair on N. Hydrogen has a duet, C and N have octets, and all formal charges are zero. H cannot be placed in the middle of an ordinary two-bond chain.

## 2.3.4 Resonance Structures
Resonance contributors have the same atom positions and connectivity but different electron placement. Move lone pairs and π electrons, not nuclei or the σ-bond skeleton. The real structure has delocalized electron density; it does not alternate between drawings.

### Worked example: nitrate, NO₃⁻
The electron budget is 5 + 3(6) + 1 = 24. An octet-respecting contributor has one N=O bond and two N–O single bonds. N has FC +1, each singly bonded O has FC −1, and the double-bonded O has FC 0. Three equivalent contributors place the double bond on different oxygens. All three N–O bonds are equivalent, with average Lewis bond order (2 + 1 + 1)/3 = 4/3.

## 2.4 Comparison: Formal Charge, Oxidation State, and Partial Charge
**Formal charge** divides bonding electrons equally. **Oxidation state** assigns bonding electrons to the more electronegative atom, dividing equally only between identical atoms. **Partial charge** describes an unequal electron-density distribution and depends on how that distribution is partitioned.

In H₂O, all formal charges are zero. Oxidation states are H = +1 and O = −2. Partial charges are H δ⁺ and O δ⁻. None of these statements means that a water molecule is a free O²⁻ ion bound to two free protons.

## Practice
::: Why does CO₃²⁻ have equivalent C–O bonds?
There are three equivalent resonance contributors. Each places one C=O bond on a different oxygen. Delocalization gives equivalent bonds, with average Lewis bond order 4/3; the nuclei do not move between contributors.
:::

::: How many valence electrons does SO₄²⁻ contain?
6 + 4(6) + 2 = 32 electrons. The −2 charge adds two electrons. Formal-charge assignments depend on the chosen Lewis representation, but they must sum to −2.
:::

::: Is a bond with zero formal charges necessarily nonpolar?
No. H–Cl has zero formal charge on each atom in its usual Lewis structure and still has a polar covalent bond because Cl attracts the bonding electron density more strongly.
:::
