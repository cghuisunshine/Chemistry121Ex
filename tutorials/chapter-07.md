# Multi-electron Species and Periodic Properties
> Add repulsion, shielding, spin, and orbital occupancy to explain the periodic table.

## 7.1 Learning Objectives
Write ground-state electron configurations for atoms and ions, apply Pauli's principle and Hund's rule, predict magnetism from unpaired electrons, and explain radius and ionization-energy trends. Identify where a simple trend has an exception and give a physical reason.

## 7.2 Multi-electron Atoms and Ions
Electron–electron repulsion changes both orbital energies and electron distributions. In an approximate orbital model, different subshells with the same n no longer have equal energies. In many atoms, penetration and shielding produce the ordering ns < np < nd within a shell.

Penetration describes how much electron density reaches close to the nucleus, inside other electrons' shielding. An s orbital can penetrate more strongly than a p orbital in the same shell. These ideas explain trends but do not replace a complete many-electron calculation.

## 7.3 Effective Nuclear Charge
An electron experiences the nucleus partly screened by other electrons. A useful qualitative expression is `Z(eff) = Z − S`, where S is a shielding estimate. Shielding depends on the orbitals involved; simply subtracting every other electron from Z is not a reliable numerical method.

Across a period, nuclear charge increases while added electrons enter the same principal shell, so valence effective nuclear attraction generally increases. Down a group, occupied shells with larger n and greater shielding usually produce larger atoms despite the larger nuclear charge.

## 7.4 Spin Quantum Number
Electron spin has two allowed projections: `mₛ = +1/2 or −1/2`. Pauli's exclusion principle says no two electrons in an atom can have the same set of all four quantum numbers. Therefore one spatial orbital holds at most two electrons, with opposite spins.

The arrows in an orbital diagram indicate spin projections, not literal clockwise and counterclockwise motion of charged balls. Three p orbitals can hold six electrons; five d orbitals can hold ten.

## 7.5 Electronic Configuration
An electron configuration records subshell occupancies, such as oxygen's `1s² 2s² 2p⁴`. The Aufbau approach fills lower-energy orbitals first. Hund's rule places electrons singly with parallel spins in degenerate orbitals before pairing them.

For oxygen, the three 2p orbitals contain one pair and two single electrons. A drawing that pairs electrons in two p orbitals while leaving the third empty violates Hund's rule for the ground state. Always distinguish a ground-state configuration from a possible excited configuration.

## 7.6 General Procedure for Writing Electronic Configurations
1. Find the electron count: atomic number minus positive charge, or plus the magnitude of negative charge.
2. Fill the neutral atom's ground-state subshells, using the usual ordering as a guide.
3. Recognize common exceptions such as Cr = [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ and Cu = [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.
4. For cations, remove electrons from the highest occupied principal shell first; transition-metal ions lose ns electrons before (n − 1)d electrons.
5. Check the total count and draw partially filled subshells to identify unpaired electrons.

### Worked example: Fe³⁺
Neutral Fe has 26 electrons and configuration [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s². Remove both 4s electrons and one 3d electron: Fe³⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁵, with 23 electrons. For the isolated-ion orbital picture, the five d electrons occupy five orbitals singly. Ligand fields in complexes can change spin patterns; that requires additional information.

### Common mistake
The common neutral-atom filling sequence does not imply that 4s electrons remain lower in energy than 3d electrons for every species. Orbital energies change with occupancy and ionization.

## 7.7 Magnetic Properties
A species with unpaired electrons is paramagnetic and is attracted to an applied magnetic field. A closed-shell species with all electrons paired is diamagnetic and is weakly repelled. Use an orbital diagram, not merely the total number of electrons, to decide.

Mg has [Ne] 3s² and is diamagnetic. Neutral N has [He] 2s² 2p³ with three unpaired electrons and is paramagnetic. An even electron count does not guarantee diamagnetism: O has eight electrons but two unpaired 2p electrons.

## 7.8 Periodic Properties
Periodic trends reflect competing effects of nuclear attraction, shielding, principal shell, and electron occupancy. A strong explanation names these causes. “It is farther right on the table” states a pattern without explaining it.

When comparing species, first ask whether they have the same electron count, the same occupied principal shell, or different charges. That choice determines which physical comparison is most useful.

## 7.8.1 Atomic and Ionic Radii
Atomic radius generally decreases across a period and increases down a group. Cations are usually smaller than their parent atoms because removing electrons reduces repulsion and may remove a whole outer shell. Anions are generally larger because added electrons increase repulsion.

### Worked example: an isoelectronic series
O²⁻, F⁻, Ne, Na⁺, and Mg²⁺ all have ten electrons. Increasing nuclear charge draws that same electron population inward, so the size order is O²⁻ > F⁻ > Ne > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ in the simple isoelectronic comparison. Experimental radius definitions vary, so compare compatible radius measures when using data.

## 7.8.2 Ionization Energy
First ionization is `X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻` and requires energy. It generally becomes harder across a period and easier down a group. Successive ionization energies increase; a large jump occurs when removal begins from a tightly bound core after the valence electrons are gone.

Common exceptions reveal subshell structure: B loses a higher-energy 2p electron more easily than Be loses a 2s electron; O loses a paired 2p electron more easily than N loses an electron from its half-filled 2p arrangement. Pairing repulsion and subshell energies qualify the broad trend.

## 7.8.3 Electron Affinity (EA)
Electron attachment is `X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g)`. Sign conventions differ: the attachment energy change is negative for exothermic attachment, while some tables report electron affinity as the positive energy released. Always state the convention before comparing signs.

Halogen attachment is often favourable, but the pattern is less regular than ionization energy. For example, attachment to Cl releases more energy than attachment to F because adding an electron to F's compact 2p shell produces stronger repulsion. Filled shells and subshell changes produce further exceptions.

## Practice
::: Give the configuration of Cu⁺ and predict its magnetism in the isolated-ion picture.
Cu is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹. Removing the 4s electron gives Cu⁺ = [Ar] 3d¹⁰. All d electrons are paired, so the isolated ion is diamagnetic.
:::

::: Which is smaller, Na⁺ or F⁻, and why?
Na⁺. Both have ten electrons, but Na⁺ has eleven protons while F⁻ has nine. The larger nuclear charge contracts the isoelectronic electron distribution.
:::

::: A main-group atom has a large jump between its second and third ionization energies. What does that suggest?
It suggests two relatively accessible valence electrons, followed by removal from a much more tightly bound core. A group 2 valence configuration is a likely interpretation for an ordinary main-group example.
:::
