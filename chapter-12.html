12.1 Learning Objectives

Workbook reference · page 12.1

Distinguish chemical identity from origin, hazard from exposure-dependent risk, and laboratory promise from demonstrated performance. Describe the broad drug-development pathway, explain molecular recognition, and compare sustainability options using a defined service and life-cycle boundary.

12.2 Natural vs. Synthetic Chemicals

Workbook reference · page 12.2

Natural and synthetic describe origin or production route; they do not by themselves determine molecular properties. Molecules with the same structure, stereochemistry, and isotopic composition have the same intrinsic chemistry regardless of origin. Real samples may still differ in impurities, concentration, or formulation.

An evidence-based comparison asks what is present, in what amount, and under which conditions. “Chemical-free” is not a literal description of a material product: water, plant oils, and air all contain chemical substances.

Worked example: two samples with the same major ingredient

If two samples contain the same molecular form of a compound but have different impurity profiles, the main molecule's identity is unchanged. Any difference in sample behaviour must be investigated through composition, physical form, concentration, and conditions rather than attributed to origin alone.

12.2.1 Organic Farming

Workbook reference · page 12.3

Organic farming refers to a production system governed by standards; “organic” in organic chemistry refers broadly to carbon chemistry. The meanings should not be substituted for one another. Organic production can still involve chemical inputs.

To compare farming systems scientifically, define outcomes such as yield, energy use, ecological effects, or measured residues, and use comparable conditions. A production label alone cannot establish the toxicity of a particular molecule or the total environmental impact per unit of food. Consult applicable standards for certification requirements rather than inferring them from chemistry terminology.

12.2.2 Toxicity

Workbook reference · page 12.4

Hazard is a substance's capacity to cause harm under specified conditions. Risk depends on the possibility and degree of harm in an exposure situation. Dose, route, duration, timing, and susceptibility affect outcomes; concentration in a container is not the same as absorbed dose.

LD₅₀ is the dose associated with death in 50% of a tested population under defined experimental conditions, often reported in mg per kg body mass. A lower LD₅₀ indicates greater acute lethality only within a suitably comparable test. It does not summarize chronic effects, every exposure route, or a safe dose.

Worked example: a classroom exposure calculation

An illustrative 2.0 mg amount corresponds to 0.040 mg kg⁻¹ for a 50 kg body mass and 0.020 mg kg⁻¹ for 100 kg. This is unit conversion only; it does not establish absorption, biological effect, or safety. Comparing toxicity data requires the species, route, endpoint, and experimental context.

12.3 Pharmaceutical Drug Development

Workbook reference · page 12.7

Drug development connects molecular hypotheses with evidence about quality, biological effects, and performance in humans. A molecule that binds a target in a test tube may fail because it does not reach that target, is rapidly degraded, has unwanted effects, or cannot be produced reliably.

Distinguish potency, the amount needed for a given effect in a specified system, from efficacy, the extent of the effect. Neither term alone establishes that a candidate is suitable for clinical use. This chapter explains concepts rather than giving treatment recommendations.

12.3.1 Drug Discovery Process

Workbook reference · page 12.7

A broad pathway includes discovery and optimization, preclinical research, clinical research, regulatory review, and continued monitoring. Preclinical work investigates activity and other properties before human studies. Clinical phases progressively address questions about safety, dose, and efficacy; the precise design depends on the product and context.

In the common phase framework, phase 1 emphasizes initial human safety and dosing, phase 2 explores efficacy and further safety, and phase 3 seeks broader confirmatory evidence. Review considers the evidence and manufacturing quality. Approval is followed by monitoring because uncommon or long-term effects may emerge later. These are broad scientific stages, not a complete regulatory checklist.

12.3.2 Mode of Action of Drugs

Workbook reference · page 12.10

A mode of action links molecular interactions to a biological effect. A drug may bind a receptor, inhibit an enzyme, alter transport, or interact with another target. Shape complementarity, charge, hydrogen bonding, dispersion, and the solvent environment can all influence binding.

Worked example: a competitive inhibitor

In a simplified reversible model, an inhibitor and substrate compete for an enzyme's active site. Greater inhibitor occupancy reduces productive substrate binding. Explaining selectivity requires comparing interactions with different targets, not merely drawing a molecule that fits one site. A binding result alone does not establish the full effect in an organism.

Stereochemistry matters because many biological targets are chiral. Two enantiomers may interact differently with a target even though their formulas and many bulk properties are the same.

12.4 Chemistry and Sustainability

Workbook reference · page 12.12

Sustainability comparisons connect chemical performance with resource use, energy, emissions, and environmental fate. Ask whether a change reduces a burden overall or simply shifts it between stages or impact categories.

A catalyst can improve a reaction by changing its pathway and reducing the activation barrier. It does not change the reaction's equilibrium constant at the same temperature. A useful sustainability claim must also consider catalyst production, recovery, lifetime, and the other process inputs.

12.4.1 Life-Cycle Assessment

Workbook reference · page 12.12

A life-cycle assessment begins with a goal, a functional unit, and a system boundary. The functional unit defines the service being compared, such as delivering 1000 L of a drink or providing 100 uses of a container. The boundary specifies which production, transport, use, and end-of-life processes are counted.

Worked example: reusable versus single-use containers

In an illustrative single-impact model, a reusable container costs 20 impact units to make and 0.2 units to wash per use. A single-use container costs 1.0 unit per use. After n uses, compare 20 + 0.2n with 1.0n. Equality occurs at n = 25; after more than 25 uses, the reusable option has lower impact in this model.

The result changes if washing energy, transport, breakage, or actual reuse changes. A real assessment examines multiple impact categories and uncertainty; these illustrative units are not measured environmental data.

12.4.2 Green Chemistry

Workbook reference · page 12.14

Green chemistry aims to reduce or eliminate hazardous substances through chemical and process design. Useful themes include waste prevention, less hazardous synthesis, safer solvents, energy efficiency, catalysis, renewable feedstocks where appropriate, and design for degradation.

Atom economy asks how much of the reactant mass could become desired product: atom economy (%) = 100 × molar mass of desired product / sum of stoichiometric reactant molar masses. Include stoichiometric coefficients. It differs from percent yield, which measures actual product relative to theoretical product.

Worked example: addition versus substitution

For ideal ethene hydrogenation, C₂H₄ + H₂ → C₂H₆, all reactant atoms appear in the desired product, so atom economy is 100%. That does not prove the process has no environmental impact: feedstocks, energy, solvents, catalysts, and real yield still matter. A high-yield reaction can also have poor atom economy if large coproduct streams are built into its stoichiometry.

12.5 What Do Chemists Do?

Workbook reference · page 12.16

Chemists measure composition, determine structures, design and synthesize materials, explain reaction pathways, model molecular behaviour, and assess chemical processes. Work spans research, manufacturing, environmental analysis, education, quality control, and many other settings.

The common skill is connecting a molecular model to evidence. A good report states the question, method, uncertainty, and conclusion, and explains which additional evidence could change the interpretation.

12.5.1 Why Choose a Degree in Chemistry?

Workbook reference · page 12.17

Chemistry develops quantitative reasoning, experimental design, data interpretation, and communication about matter and change. To explore whether it suits your interests, compare the kinds of problems you enjoy: making molecules, measuring trace substances, explaining physical behaviour, or designing materials.

Use your course's research features and official program information to investigate possible study paths. Program requirements and career conditions change; this tutorial supplies a framework for reflection rather than a current admissions or employment guide.

Does “natural” establish that a substance is less toxic than a synthetic substance?Think first, then open the explanation

No. Compare chemical identity, composition, dose, route, duration, and relevant evidence. Origin alone does not determine toxic effects.

Is 95% yield equivalent to 95% atom economy?Think first, then open the explanation

No. Yield compares actual with theoretical desired product. Atom economy compares the desired product's stoichiometric mass with the mass of reactants incorporated into the balanced reaction. A process can score highly on one measure and poorly on the other.

What must be fixed before comparing two packaging options by LCA?Think first, then open the explanation

Define the service or functional unit and a consistent system boundary. Then compare compatible data for production, transport, use, and end of life, including realistic reuse or recovery assumptions and relevant impact categories.
