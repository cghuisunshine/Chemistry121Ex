3.1 Learning Objectives

Workbook reference · page 3.1

Draw a correct Lewis structure, count electron domains at each central atom, distinguish electron-domain geometry from molecular shape, estimate bond angles, and determine whether the bond-dipole vectors cancel. Explain your prediction using both geometry and atom identity.

3.2 Molecular Shape

Workbook reference · page 3.2

Molecular shape describes the positions of nuclei in three dimensions. A flat Lewis drawing normally gives connectivity rather than the actual bond angles. Electron-domain geometry includes both bonds and lone pairs; molecular shape names the arrangement of bonded atoms only.

For NH₃, four electron domains give a tetrahedral electron-domain arrangement, while three bonded atoms and one lone pair give a trigonal pyramidal molecular shape. Writing only “tetrahedral” loses this distinction.

3.3 How to Determine Molecular Shape Using the VSEPR Model

Workbook reference · page 3.3

VSEPR treats regions of valence electron density as repelling one another. A single, double, or triple bond counts as one domain in the direction of that bond. A lone pair counts as one domain. In AXₘEₙ, A is the central atom, X counts bonded atoms, and E counts lone pairs.

  • 2 domains: linear, 180°; AX₂ is linear.
  • 3 domains: trigonal planar, 120°; AX₂E is bent.
  • 4 domains: tetrahedral, 109.5°; AX₃E is trigonal pyramidal and AX₂E₂ is bent.
  • 5 domains: trigonal bipyramidal, 90°, 120°, and 180°; AX₄E is seesaw, AX₃E₂ is T-shaped, and AX₂E₃ is linear.
  • 6 domains: octahedral, 90° and 180°; AX₅E is square pyramidal and AX₄E₂ is square planar.

Lone pairs generally compress adjacent bond angles. In trigonal bipyramidal arrangements, lone pairs preferentially occupy equatorial positions because those have fewer 90° interactions. In AX₄E₂ octahedral arrangements, the lone pairs lie opposite one another.

Worked example: water

H₂O has eight valence electrons. Its oxygen has two O–H bonds and two lone pairs, so it is AX₂E₂: tetrahedral electron-domain geometry and bent molecular shape. The measured H–O–H angle is about 104.5°, smaller than 109.5°. VSEPR explains the trend but does not calculate an exact experimental angle.

Worked example: XeF₄

XeF₄ has 8 + 4(7) = 36 valence electrons. After four Xe–F bonds and terminal F octets, four electrons remain as two lone pairs on Xe. Six domains give an octahedral arrangement; opposite lone pairs leave four F atoms in a square plane. The molecular shape is square planar.

Common mistake

Do not count a double bond as two separate domains. CO₂ has two domains at carbon and is linear. Its four bonding electron pairs do not make it tetrahedral.

3.4 Shapes of Larger Molecules

Workbook reference · page 3.16

Apply VSEPR locally to every atom of interest. A molecule can contain several local geometries and does not need one global VSEPR name. In ethanol, CH₃CH₂OH, both carbon atoms are approximately tetrahedral and oxygen has a bent local arrangement.

For ethene, H₂C=CH₂, each carbon has three domains and a trigonal planar local arrangement. The π bond restricts rotation about C=C; rotating one end breaks the favourable parallel p-orbital overlap. Around an ordinary C–C single bond, rotation is often much easier.

3.5 Molecular Polarity

Workbook reference · page 3.20

A permanent molecular dipole is the vector sum of contributions from the charge distribution. For many simple examples, adding bond-dipole vectors gives a useful prediction. First identify polar bonds, then use the actual three-dimensional shape to check cancellation.

Worked comparison

  • CO₂: two equal C=O dipoles point in opposite directions; the linear molecule has no net dipole.
  • H₂O: two O–H dipoles meet at a bent angle and reinforce along the angle bisector; water is polar.
  • BF₃: three equal B–F dipoles cancel in a trigonal plane.
  • CH₂Cl₂: tetrahedral geometry does not guarantee cancellation when substituents differ; the molecule is polar.
  • XeF₄: equal Xe–F dipoles cancel in the square plane, despite lone pairs on Xe.

Common mistake

“Contains polar bonds” and “has lone pairs” are not sufficient tests for a molecular dipole. Symmetry and the identities of all substituents determine whether the contributions cancel.

3.6 Chemistry at UBC

Workbook reference · page 3.25

Connect structure prediction to research questions: how does changing a ligand alter a metal complex's shape, how can a polymer's structure affect its properties, or how can spectroscopy test a proposed molecule? These are applications of the chapter's models rather than a summary of a particular research group's work.

3.7 How Do Chemists Determine the Structure of a New Molecule?

Workbook reference · page 3.25

Chemists combine independent evidence. Elemental analysis and mass spectrometry constrain composition and mass. Infrared spectroscopy identifies characteristic vibrations. NMR provides information about local chemical environments and connectivity. X-ray diffraction can reveal atomic positions in a suitable crystal.

VSEPR predicts a plausible arrangement once connectivity is known; it does not independently prove a molecular structure. A good proposal explains all the available evidence, including results that distinguish between possible isomers.

↗ Practice questions

Predict the shape and polarity of SO₂.Think first, then open the explanation

In a common octet-based description, S has two bonding directions and one lone pair: three domains, trigonal planar electron-domain geometry, and bent molecular shape. The S–O dipoles do not cancel, so SO₂ is polar. Alternative resonance drawings do not change the number of bonding directions.

Why is SF₄ seesaw rather than tetrahedral?Think first, then open the explanation

Its central S has four bonds and one lone pair: five domains, not four. The electron-domain arrangement is trigonal bipyramidal. Putting the lone pair equatorial leaves a seesaw arrangement of F atoms.

Compare the local carbon geometries in CH₃–C≡N.Think first, then open the explanation

The CH₃ carbon has four domains and is approximately tetrahedral. The nitrile carbon has two domains, one toward CH₃ and one toward N, and is linear. The triple bond counts as one domain.
