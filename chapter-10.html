Conjugation and Colour

Follow π-electron delocalization from orbital overlap to absorption and the colour of materials.

10.1 Learning Objectives

Workbook reference · page 10.1

Identify continuous conjugated pathways, fill simple π MO diagrams, relate a frontier-orbital gap to absorbed wavelength, predict qualitative effects of extending conjugation, and interpret skeletal, perspective, and condensed structures. Distinguish absorbed light from the light that reaches the observer.

10.2 Introduction

Workbook reference · page 10.2

The arrangement of bonds controls the possible electron states. Connecting adjacent p orbitals allows π electrons to delocalize over more than one bond, changing energies, lengths, and reactivity. Some energy gaps fall within the visible range, giving coloured materials.

Not every conjugated molecule is visibly coloured. Small conjugated systems often absorb ultraviolet radiation. Visible appearance also depends on concentration, illumination, the breadth of absorption bands, and whether the sample transmits or reflects light.

10.3 Definition of Conjugation

Workbook reference · page 10.3

A conjugated pathway contains a continuous set of adjacent orbitals, usually p orbitals, capable of overlapping. Alternating double and single bonds are a common pattern, but a suitably oriented lone pair, empty p orbital, or radical centre can also participate.

In CH₂=CH–CH=CH₂, all four carbons have p orbitals in a continuous pathway. In CH₂=CH–CH₂–CH=CH₂, the tetrahedral CH₂ interrupts the ordinary p-orbital pathway. Geometry matters: twisting adjacent p orbitals toward perpendicular alignment reduces conjugation.

10.4 Consequences of π Conjugated Systems

Workbook reference · page 10.3

Delocalization can lower the overall electronic energy relative to an appropriate localized comparison, redistribute bond lengths, and give an intervening single bond partial double-bond character. Restricted rotation can favour conformations with useful p overlap.

Within a comparable family, extending the conjugated system commonly decreases the HOMO–LUMO gap and shifts absorption toward longer wavelengths. Substituents, charge, geometry, and environment also matter, so “more conjugation” is a qualitative guide rather than a universal numerical rule.

10.5 Understanding the Bonding of 1,3-Butadiene

Workbook reference · page 10.6

Butadiene, CH₂=CH–CH=CH₂, has four approximately sp² carbons. Each contributes one p orbital and one π electron. The σ framework establishes connectivity; the four p orbitals combine into a four-orbital π system containing four electrons.

The central C–C bond can support conjugation because the atoms on both sides possess p orbitals. A drawn single bond between double bonds therefore does not imply a tetrahedral interruption.

10.5.1 Building the MOs of 1,3-Butadiene

Workbook reference · page 10.6

Four interacting p orbitals form four π MOs. In the simple linear-chain model, their phases can be represented from lowest to highest energy as follows; these signs show phase only, not equal coefficient magnitudes:

  • ψ₁: + + + +, zero internal sign changes.
  • ψ₂: + + − −, one sign change.
  • ψ₃: + − − +, two sign changes.
  • ψ₄: + − + −, three sign changes.

The four π electrons fill ψ₁ and ψ₂ with two electrons each. ψ₂ is the highest occupied molecular orbital, or HOMO; ψ₃ is the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital, or LUMO. More internuclear nodes generally correspond to higher energy in this simple series.

Worked example: identifying the excitation

The simplest frontier-orbital picture promotes one electron from ψ₂ to ψ₃. The required photon energy is approximately E(LUMO) − E(HOMO), not the absolute energy of either orbital. Real spectra additionally depend on electron interactions and whether a transition is optically allowed.

10.5.2 Using Particle-in-a-Box Model

Workbook reference · page 10.13

Treat a conjugated π pathway as an approximate one-dimensional box of effective length L. The levels are Eₙ = n²h²/(8mₑL²), each holding two electrons in this model. For four electrons, n = 1 and n = 2 are filled, so the frontier transition is 2 → 3.

Worked example: an illustrative four-electron box

For L = 0.60 nm, h²/(8mₑL²) ≈ 1.04 eV. The 2 → 3 gap is (9 − 4)(1.04) = 5.22 eV, giving λ ≈ 238 nm, in the ultraviolet. This is a model estimate, not a measured butadiene wavelength. The effective box length and neglected interactions limit accuracy.

For a fixed electron count, increasing L reduces the gap as 1/L². When comparing different polyenes, the electron count also changes, so update the occupied levels as well as L.

10.6 Chemical Origins of Colour

Workbook reference · page 10.17

A visible colour can arise when a material absorbs selected parts of visible light. In many organic dyes, conjugated electronic states provide the relevant transitions. Other materials can be coloured by metal-centred transitions, charge transfer, scattering, or interference.

Use λ = hc/ΔE to connect an electronic gap to a photon wavelength. A smaller positive gap corresponds to a longer wavelength. Colour cannot be inferred from the presence of a π bond alone.

10.6.1 Colour Vision

Workbook reference · page 10.17

Human colour vision compares responses of cone cells with overlapping sensitivities to different parts of the visible spectrum. A perceived colour can result from many different spectral distributions; it need not correspond to a single wavelength.

The illumination and viewing conditions matter. A sample cannot reflect a wavelength absent from its light source. Chemistry predicts which light interacts with the sample; perception also depends on the observer and surroundings.

10.6.2 Subtractive Colour Theory

Workbook reference · page 10.19

A dye or filter removes parts of incident light, leaving the transmitted or reflected remainder. In a rough complementary-colour description, preferential absorption of orange light can leave a blue appearance. This differs from adding red, green, and blue light sources.

Mixing absorbing dyes usually removes more wavelength regions and often produces a darker result. Predicting an exact perceived colour needs the full absorption spectrum and illumination, not only a single absorption maximum.

10.6.3 Determining the Colour of Extended π Systems

Workbook reference · page 10.20

Use a sequence: identify the conjugated pathway, estimate whether the energy gap falls in the visible, determine the absorbed region, then consider the remaining light. A red shift means the absorption moves to longer wavelength; it does not by itself mean the sample looks red.

For example, an illustrative transition with ΔE = 2.48 eV absorbs near 500 nm. Under white light, removing part of the green region can give a roughly complementary magenta appearance if that absorption dominates. Broad and multiple bands can change the result.

10.6.4 Applications: Organic Dyes

Workbook reference · page 10.24

A chromophore is the structural part responsible for characteristic absorption. Electron-donating and electron-withdrawing substituents can change orbital energies and alter a conjugated dye's spectrum. Protonation can also change which lone pairs participate in conjugation, explaining the colour changes of some indicators.

For a structure–colour comparison, identify what changed chemically before predicting a spectral shift. Dye attachment, solubility, aggregation, and photostability affect how well a chromophore functions in a real material.

10.7 Natural Dyeing

Workbook reference · page 10.27

Natural dyes are obtained from biological or mineral sources. Their colour still follows chemical structure and light absorption. Their attachment to a fibre may involve intermolecular attractions or coordination to a mordant, which can also alter the dye's electronic environment.

Compare a dye before and after a pH change or interaction with a metal ion: identify the structural or coordination change and then connect it to absorption. Natural origin alone does not establish safety or environmental performance; those require evidence about composition, exposure, and the full process.

10.8 Line-bond Drawings

Workbook reference · page 10.29

In skeletal drawings, each unlabeled vertex or line end usually represents carbon. Hydrogens attached to carbon are omitted and inferred from ordinary valence; heteroatoms and their attached hydrogens are generally shown. Multiple bonds remain explicit.

A three-segment open zigzag contains four carbons, not three. To decode a structure, label all vertices and ends, count existing bond orders at each carbon, and add enough hydrogens to give its expected valence. Charges and unusual valences must be shown explicitly.

10.9 Line-bond Perspective Diagrams

Workbook reference · page 10.30

A plain line lies approximately in the page, a solid wedge points toward the viewer, and a hashed wedge points away. These conventions communicate three-dimensional orientation while preserving connectivity.

Redrawing a molecule from another viewpoint does not automatically change its identity. Rotating a whole tetrahedral centre preserves its configuration; exchanging just two substituents changes the configuration when four different substituents are present. Use a model if a two-dimensional drawing is ambiguous.

10.10 Condensed Structures

Workbook reference · page 10.31

Condensed formulas group atoms to communicate connectivity more compactly than a full drawing. CH₃CH₂OH represents ethanol, whereas CH₃OCH₃ represents dimethyl ether. Both have formula C₂H₆O but different connectivity.

Parentheses mark branches: (CH₃)₂CHCl has two CH₃ groups attached to a CH bearing Cl. Expand a condensed formula before assigning hybridization, geometry, or conjugation; atom totals alone cannot distinguish isomers.

↗ Practice questions

Is CH₂=CH–O–CH₃ potentially conjugated?Think first, then open the explanation

Yes. An oxygen lone pair can occupy an orbital aligned with the adjacent alkene p orbitals. Conjugation does not require a drawn alternating sequence of double bonds at every position; suitable lone pairs can participate.

A gap falls from 3.1 eV to 2.5 eV. How does its absorption wavelength change?Think first, then open the explanation

Using λ ≈ 1240/ΔE in nm gives about 400 nm initially and 496 nm afterward. The absorption shifts to longer wavelength, a red shift. The perceived sample colour needs a separate assessment.

In a six-π-electron box, which levels are HOMO and LUMO?Think first, then open the explanation

Two electrons fill each spatial level, so n = 1, 2, and 3 are occupied. The HOMO is n = 3 and the LUMO is n = 4; the elementary frontier transition has gap (16 − 9)h²/(8mₑL²).
