Molecular Orbital Theory

Combine atomic wavefunctions into molecular states to predict bond order and magnetism.

Original study notes aligned to the chapter and section headings in the 2024–2025 resource package table of contents. Page labels refer to the workbook. Use your workbook and course materials for the assigned learning objectives, research features, and exercises.

8.1 Learning Objectives

Workbook reference · page 8.1

Explain bonding and antibonding combinations, count molecular orbitals, fill MO diagrams using electron count and spin rules, calculate bond order, and predict unpaired electrons. Recognize the different valence MO orderings used for lighter and heavier second-row homonuclear diatomics.

8.2 Why MO Theory and VBT Are Needed

Workbook reference · page 8.2

Lewis structures and VSEPR provide efficient electron bookkeeping and shape predictions. They do not directly describe orbital energies, phase, or the quantum distribution of electrons in a molecule. Valence bond theory emphasizes localized overlap, while molecular orbital theory uses orbitals extending over the molecule.

These are models with different strengths. An MO treatment explains why O₂ has unpaired electrons even though its ordinary Lewis drawing has all electrons paired. Chapter 9 uses VBT to connect local geometry with σ and π bonding.

8.3 Limitations of Lewis and VSEPR Theories

Workbook reference · page 8.2

A Lewis bond line does not specify a spatial wavefunction. VSEPR does not give an electronic energy-level diagram or directly calculate magnetic behaviour and spectra. Electron-deficient and delocalized species can also require more than a single Lewis contributor.

Use the simplest model that answers the question, then identify what it leaves unresolved. “O₂ has a double bond” is a useful Lewis description; it is not a complete account of the molecule's spin state.

8.4 Wave Superposition

Workbook reference · page 8.3

In a simple linear combination of atomic orbitals, ψ(bonding) ∝ φA + φB and ψ(antibonding) ∝ φA − φB, with appropriate normalization and phase conventions. Constructive overlap increases density between nuclei; destructive overlap creates an internuclear node and gives an antibonding orbital.

Two atomic orbitals generate two molecular orbitals; the number of orbitals is conserved. Effective interaction requires compatible symmetry, meaningful spatial overlap, and reasonably close atomic-orbital energies. Orbital colours indicate phase, not positive and negative electric charge.

8.5 Molecular Orbitals of Molecular Hydrogen (H₂)

Workbook reference · page 8.7

The two H 1s orbitals form a lower-energy σ1s orbital and a higher-energy σ*1s orbital. The asterisk denotes antibonding. H₂'s two electrons fill the lower orbital with opposite spins, giving (σ1s)².

The bonding orbital has increased electron density between the nuclei relative to the separated-orbital picture. Its occupation stabilizes the molecule near its equilibrium separation. “Adding amplitudes” must include normalization; it does not create extra electrons.

8.6 Brief Summary of MO Theory

Workbook reference · page 8.8

Molecular orbitals belong to the molecule as a whole. Each spatial MO can hold two opposite-spin electrons. Fill lower levels before higher levels, and distribute electrons singly across degenerate orbitals before pairing.

The elementary MO bond-order expression is bond order = (N(bonding) − N(antibonding))/2. Nonbonding electrons do not contribute to this expression. A positive bond order suggests net bonding in the model; a zero value does not rule out every weak intermolecular association.

8.7 Energy Levels of MOs in Hydrogen

Workbook reference · page 8.9

An MO diagram shows atomic orbitals on the sides and molecular levels in the middle. The bonding σ1s lies below the parent 1s levels, while σ*1s lies above. The exact level energies depend on internuclear separation.

Worked comparison: H₂⁺, H₂, and He₂

  • H₂⁺: one bonding electron, bond order 1/2, one unpaired electron.
  • H₂: two bonding electrons, bond order 1, no unpaired electrons.
  • He₂ in this simple covalent MO model: two bonding and two antibonding electrons, bond order 0, all paired.

Within a comparable series, lower bond order generally means a longer, weaker bond. Do not interpret level diagrams as literal electron trajectories between nuclei.

8.8 Procedure for Filling MO Diagrams

Workbook reference · page 8.12

  1. Count electrons, adjusting for charge. Decide whether the diagram includes all electrons or valence electrons only.
  2. Choose the MO ordering appropriate to the species.
  3. Fill from low to high energy, applying Pauli's principle and Hund's rule.
  4. Count bonding and antibonding electrons consistently.
  5. Calculate bond order and count unpaired electrons for the magnetic prediction.

Core bonding/antibonding pairs often cancel in bond-order calculations for second-row species. If you omit them, omit both consistently. Never mix an all-electron count with a valence-only diagram.

8.9 Molecular Orbitals of Second-Row Homonuclear Diatomic Species

Workbook reference · page 8.14

Take the internuclear axis as z. The 2p𝓏 orbitals combine end-on to give σ2p and σ*2p; the 2pₓ and 2pᵧ orbitals combine side-on to give two degenerate π2p orbitals and two degenerate π*2p orbitals.

The usual valence ordering for B₂, C₂, and N₂ is σ2s < σ*2s < π2p < σ2p < π*2p < σ*2p. For O₂ and F₂ it is σ2s < σ*2s < σ2p < π2p < π*2p < σ*2p. Mixing of orbitals with the same symmetry helps explain the change. Check the diagram convention supplied by your course.

Worked example: N₂

N₂ has ten valence electrons: two in σ2s, two in σ*2s, four across the π2p pair, and two in σ2p. There are eight bonding and two antibonding valence electrons, giving bond order 3. All are paired, so N₂ is diamagnetic.

8.9.1 Molecular Orbitals of Dilithium (Li₂)

Workbook reference · page 8.15

Each Li contributes one 2s valence electron. Li₂ has (σ2s)² in a valence-only diagram, giving bond order 1 and no unpaired electrons. If the 1s core is included, (σ1s)²(σ*1s)² contributes zero net bond order.

Ionizing Li₂ to Li₂⁺ removes a bonding electron, lowering the bond order to 1/2. The predicted bond becomes weaker and generally longer, and the remaining electron is unpaired.

8.9.2 Molecular Orbitals of Dioxygen (O₂)

Workbook reference · page 8.16

O₂ has twelve valence electrons. After filling σ2s, σ*2s, σ2p, and both π2p orbitals, two electrons remain. Hund's rule places them singly in the two degenerate π*2p orbitals. O₂ is therefore paramagnetic, with bond order (8 − 4)/2 = 2.

Worked example: oxygen ions

O₂⁺ loses one antibonding electron, giving bond order 2.5 and one unpaired electron. O₂⁻ gains one antibonding electron, giving bond order 1.5 and one unpaired electron. O₂²⁻ has two additional antibonding electrons, bond order 1, and all electrons paired. The expected bond-length order is O₂⁺ < O₂ < O₂⁻ < O₂²⁻.

↗ Practice questions

Why can removing an electron strengthen O₂?Think first, then open the explanation

The electron is removed from an antibonding π* orbital. Decreasing antibonding occupancy increases the bond order from 2 to 2.5 in O₂⁺.

Predict B₂'s bond order and magnetism using the lighter-diatomic ordering.Think first, then open the explanation

Its six valence electrons occupy (σ2s)²(σ*2s)²(π2p)². Hund's rule puts the last two in separate π orbitals. Bond order is 1 and there are two unpaired electrons, so B₂ is paramagnetic.

How many MOs arise from six interacting 2p atomic orbitals?Think first, then open the explanation

Six: one σ bonding, two π bonding, two π antibonding, and one σ antibonding orbital. Degeneracy does not reduce the number of orbitals.
